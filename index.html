Climate Crossroads

Projected warming relative to 1850-1900 under four emissions scenarios. Lines show the median of 7 CMIP6 models, shaded bands show the model range. Annual means, area-weighted over IPCC AR6 land regions, 10-year smoothing.

See our checkpoint page and exploratory graphs

Every region on Earth shares the same climate past, but its future depends on how much greenhouse gas the world emits from here on. This chart shows how much warmer a region gets compared to the late 1800s, before most human emissions. The gray line is what models simulate for the past. At 2015 it splits into four possible futures, one for each emissions scenario.

How to read it:

  • Lines show the middle estimate (the median of 7 climate models).
  • Shaded bands show the full range across those models. A wider band means the models disagree more.
  • The dashed +2°C line marks a key warming threshold, so you can see when each path crosses it.
  • Pick a region from the dropdown or the quick picks, turn scenarios on or off with the buttons, and hover over the chart to see exact values for any year.

What do the scenarios mean?

Each scenario is a "Shared Socioeconomic Pathway" (SSP), a story about how the world might develop and how much it emits. The first number is the pathway (1 to 5). The second number is how much extra heat the atmosphere traps by 2100, in watts per square meter. Bigger means more warming.

SSP1-2.6: Low emissions

The world moves quickly toward clean energy and sustainability. Emissions drop sharply and reach net zero in the second half of the century. This is the closest to the Paris Agreement goal of keeping global warming well below 2°C.

SSP2-4.5: Middle of the road

Trends continue roughly as they have. Emissions stay near today's levels until about mid-century, then slowly decline, but don't reach zero by 2100.

SSP3-7.0: High emissions

Countries focus on their own security and cooperate less. Emissions keep rising and roughly double by 2100.

SSP5-8.5: Very high emissions

Rapid economic growth powered mostly by fossil fuels. Emissions roughly double by 2050 and stay very high. This is the worst case the models simulate.

Note: these are warming numbers for land regions. Land heats up faster than the ocean, so regional numbers here are often higher than the global averages you see in the news.

Write-up

Motivation

Most climate graphics show one global number, which makes warming feel abstract. We wanted to answer a more personal question: how much hotter will a specific part of the world get, and how much does that depend on the emissions path we take? CMIP6 lets us compare the same regions under four different futures, so we built a chart where the shared past visibly splits into those futures at 2015.

Design rationale

Visual encodings. We used a line chart because the main thing to read is change over time, and position along a common scale is the most accurate way to compare values. Each scenario is one line (the median of 7 models), and the shaded band around it shows the full range across models. We wanted the uncertainty to be visible instead of hiding it behind a single line. The dashed line at 2015 marks where the historical runs end and the scenarios begin, and the dashed +2°C line gives readers a reference point to judge each path against.

Color. The scenarios use an ordered palette from green (low emissions) through orange to dark red (very high emissions), so color follows the amount of warming. The historical line is gray because it is shared by every future. We also label the end of each line by name, so the chart can be read without relying on color alone.

Data transformations. We converted monthly temperature to annual means, averaged grid cells within each IPCC AR6 land region (weighted by cos(latitude)), and expressed everything as warming relative to each model's own 1850-1900 average. That baseline removes the differences between models that simply run warmer or cooler. We then took the median and min/max across 7 models, applied a 10-year rolling mean to smooth year-to-year noise, and cut the data at 2100 because only a few models run past it. These steps are summarized in the subtitle at the top of the page.

Interactions. The scenario buttons let readers take out or add back any of the climate paths, so they can isolate the futures they care about and compare them directly. This also fixes overplotting: with all four bands on, the low and middle paths overlap, and the y-axis rescales to fit whatever is shown. The "Try:" links guide readers to the regions that are most useful to look at, like the fastest- and slowest-warming regions, instead of making them sift through all 46 regions in the dropdown. The summary text under the chart is also reactive: it updates with each region, so readers can read about the gap between paths and when each one crosses +2°C while looking at the graph. Hovering shows exact values and the model range for any year. We also replaced the raw IPCC region codes (like "W.North-America") with plain names (like "Western North America") in the dropdown, the "Try:" links, and the summary text, to make the page easier to read, especially for those who do not understand the shortened region names, and make the design more cohesive.

Alternatives we considered. We considered a choropleth map with a year slider, which would show geography better but makes it hard to compare scenarios over time. We also considered small multiples (one panel per region), but 46 panels would be too small to read. We chose one detailed chart with a region selector and used our exploratory graphs page for the regional overview.

Development process

Team split. Chaylen handled the technical side of the project, including the data processing in Colab and building the D3 chart and its interactions, with some technical help from Jeffrey. Jeffrey also recorded and put together the checkpoint video. Peter, Jayleen, and Jeffrey contributed to the write-ups, and Peter and Jayleen helped shape the project's direction and gave feedback on the design as it developed.

Time. We spent roughly 15 people-hours in total. The preprocessing took the most time. CMIP6 is a very large dataset, so a lot of work went into exploring it to find the files we needed, making sure all 7 models loaded correctly, and getting the full pipeline to run without errors. Once the data was ready, the D3 side took the next most time. Getting the hover tooltip to work and keeping the lines, bands, and labels in sync when scenarios are toggled on and off was a bit of a struggle.
