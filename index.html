Climate Crossroads

See our checkpoint page and exploratory graphs

Every region has warmed along a similar path in the past, but where this path goes next depends on how much greenhouse gas the world keeps on emitting. The gray line shows our past before the rise of human emissions, and how much a region has warmed since the 1800s. In 2015, the visualization splits into four colored paths. Each path represents a "what if" scenario that climate scientists use to imagine how the world's global emissions could develop depending on the actions humans take to alleviate or worsen climate change. This can range from quickly switching to clean energy resources to, on the flip side, relying heavily on fossil fuels. Each of the different models shows how much warmer a certain region would get under each one of these scenarios, so you can see how much our choices change its future.

How to read it:

  • Lines show the middle estimate of the 7 climate models' predictions for each of the scenarios (the median of 7 climate models).
  • Shaded bands show the full range across those models. A wider band means the models disagree more, and a smaller band shows more agreement between models.
  • The dashed horizontal +2°C line marks a key warming threshold. Past this point, scientists expect much more severe impacts on the climate, such as heat waves and droughts, so you can see when each path crosses it.
  • Pick a region from the dropdown, or choose one of the quick selection picks below it. Turn the colored scenario buttons (SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, or SSP5-8.5) on or off to compare how each climate path affects that region. Hover over the chart to see exact values of warming for any year.

What do the scenarios mean?

Each scenario is called a "Shared Socioeconomic Pathway" (SSP). Each one is a possible story for the future, like how fast the population grows, how the economy develops, and how much countries rely on clean energy sources vs fossil fuels. Scientists around the world use these same stories/baselines, so their results can be compared accurately and fairly.

SSP1-2.6: Low emissions

The world moves quickly toward clean energy and sustainability. Emissions drop sharply and reach net zero in the second half of the century. Of the four scenarios shown here, this is the closest to the Paris Agreement goal of keeping global warming well below 2°C.

SSP2-4.5: Middle of the road

Trends continue roughly as they have. Emissions stay near today's levels until about mid-century, then slowly decline, but don't reach zero by 2100.

SSP3-7.0: High emissions

Countries focus on their own security and cooperate less. Emissions keep rising and roughly double by 2100.

SSP5-8.5: Very high emissions

Rapid economic growth powered mostly by fossil fuels. Emissions roughly double by 2050 and stay very high. This is the worst case the models simulate.

Note: These statistics cover the warming of land regions. Land heats up faster than the ocean, so regional numbers here are often higher than the global averages that include the ocean.

Sources for the scenario descriptions: IPCC Sixth Assessment Report, Working Group I (2021); "The UN climate report's five futures, decoded" (Reuters); Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (Wikipedia).

Write-up

Motivation

Many climate graphics online and in other sources show static visualizations with numbers and figures, often making global warming feel abstract and far away. Climate change is a prominent issue, and so we wanted to create a story that can urge people to act now in order to change a worsened climate future. To address this, we want to explore the question: how much hotter will specific parts of the world become? This would help us envision which regions are better or worse at reducing their effects of climate change and compare these regions against each other. We answered this question using CMIP6's four different futures and made a chart showing how the past climate outcomes split into the hypothetical scenarios in the future.

Design rationale

Visual encodings. We used a line chart because we need to encode changes over time, and so position along a common scale is the best way. Each scenario is a different colored line, and the shaded band around each one shows the full variance across models. We did this since we wanted the uncertainty to be visible. The dashed line starting at 2015 marks where the historical data ends and the hypothetical scenarios begin, while the +2°C reference line gives readers something to compare each path with.

Color. The scenarios use an ordered palette from green to dark red, with more red colors signalling more warming. We use contrasting colors in order to distinguish the climate paths easily and used typical cultural signifiers, such as green being good (or go in typical U.S. stoplights) and red being bad (or stop in U.S. stoplights). The historical line is gray because this information came from the past, and there is no new information to be gained. This acts as a baseline to the climate paths. We also label the end of each line by name, so each line can be followed to its scenario name.

Data transformations. We averaged monthly temperature to yearly means, averaged grid cells within each IPCC AR6 land region weighted by cos(latitude), and expressed everything as warming growth relative to each model's own 1850-1900 average. That baseline removes the differences between models that are simply overall warmer or cooler. We then took the median, min, and max across 7 models, applied a 10-year rolling mean to smooth year-to-year noise, and cut the data at 2100 because only a few models run past it.

Interactions. The scenario buttons let readers toggle the climate paths so they can isolate what they're interested in looking at. This also fixes overplotting. With all four bands on, the low and middle paths overlap, and the y-axis scales to fit whatever is shown. The "Try:" links guide readers to the regions that are most interesting to look at, like the fastest and slowest regions in terms of warming growth. This is better than having them sift through all 46 regions to find that information. The summary text under the chart also updates with each region, so readers can see for themselves the gap between paths and notably when each one crosses the +2°C reference line. Hovering a point in time shows exact values and the model range for any year. We also replaced the raw IPCC region codes with plain names. For example, we changed W.North-America to Western North America for readers.

Alternatives we considered. We considered a choropleth map with a year slider, which would show geography better. However, a choropleth map makes it very hard to compare scenarios over time neatly and efficiently. We also considered small multiples panels per region, but 46 panels is too many to display without the individual panels becoming too small. Therefore, we chose one detailed chart with a region selector and used our exploratory graphs page for the regional overview.

Development process

Team split. Chaylen mostly handled the technical side of the project with some help from Peter, which includes the data processing and building the D3 chart. Jeffrey also recorded and put together the checkpoint video. Peter, Jayleen, and Jeffrey contributed to the write-ups.

Time. We spent roughly 15 hours in total. The preprocessing took a lot of time since CMIP6 is a very large dataset. A lot of work went into exploring the dataset to find the files we needed and making sure all 7 models loaded correctly, as well as the full pipeline running correctly. After that, the D3 side took the next most time. Getting the hover tooltip to work and getting all of the lines, bands, and labels to agree when scenarios are toggled were tasks we struggled with.
