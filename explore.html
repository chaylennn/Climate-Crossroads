2. Dataset

We are using CMIP6 (Coupled Model Intercomparison Project, Phase 6) climate model simulations from the NOAA public dataset on Google Cloud. CMIP6 contains output from dozens of climate models run by research centers around the world, under a shared set of experiments.

What we use from it:

  • Variable: tas, near-surface air temperature, at monthly resolution (Amon table).
  • Experiments: historical (1850 to 2014) and four future scenarios (2015 to 2100): SSP1-2.6 (low emissions), SSP2-4.5 (middle), SSP3-7.0 (high), and SSP5-8.5 (very high).
  • Models: 7 models that have all five experiments: ACCESS-CM2, CanESM5, GFDL-ESM4, IPSL-CM6A-LR, MIROC6, MPI-ESM1-2-LR, and MRI-ESM2-0 (first ensemble member, r1i1p1f1, for each).
  • Regions: the 46 IPCC AR6 land regions.

How we transformed it (in preprocessing.ipynb):

  • Converted Kelvin to °C and averaged monthly values into annual means.
  • Averaged each region's grid cells, weighted by cos(latitude) so cells near the poles don't count too much.
  • Kept years up to 2100, since only a few models run past it.
  • Converted temperatures to warming relative to each model's own 1850-1900 average.
  • Combined the 7 models into a median, plus the min and max as an uncertainty range.
  • Applied a 10-year centered rolling mean to smooth year-to-year noise.

The result is one small CSV (region, scenario, year, median, low, high) that D3 loads directly.

3. Exploratory graphs

All graphs below are built with D3 from the same processed CSV.

Graph 1. One past, four futures

Average of all 46 land regions (unweighted mean of region medians). Shaded bands show the model range.

Why it matters: every scenario shares the same history, then splits apart after 2015. The future depends on which path we take.

Graph 2. Which regions warm the most?

Mean warming over 2081-2100 under SSP5-8.5, ranked.

Why it matters: warming is not the same everywhere, so a single global number hides a lot. This pushed us toward letting users pick a region.

Graph 3. Where does the choice of future matter most?

Mean warming over 2081-2100 under low (SSP1-2.6) vs very high (SSP5-8.5) emissions, sorted by the gap between them.

Why it matters: the gap between scenarios is the cost of the path we choose, and it is much larger in some regions than others.

Graph 4. When does each region pass +2°C?

First year the smoothed median reaches 2°C of warming, under each scenario. Hover a dot for the exact year.

Why it matters: the scenario changes not just how much warming happens, but when a region hits a threshold, or whether it hits it at all by 2100.

Graph 5. Warming decade by decade

Mean warming per decade for every region. Switch scenarios to compare; the color scale stays fixed so colors are comparable. Hover a cell for the value.

Why it matters: switching scenarios here shows how fast the differences build up over time, which a single snapshot year can't show.

Graph 6. Do the models agree?

Each dot is a region. x: median warming over 2081-2100 under SSP5-8.5. y: model spread (max minus min across the 7 models). Hover for the region name.

Why it matters: models disagree more in some places than others, so our final chart shows a range, not just one line.

4. Our question

How much do different emission paths change the future of where you live?

Working title: Climate Crossroads. The graphs above show that (1) every region shares the same past but the scenarios split sharply after 2015, (2) how much warming happens, and when, depends heavily on location, and (3) the models agree on the direction but not the exact amount. Our final graph lets a user pick a region and compare the paths directly, with the model range shown.
